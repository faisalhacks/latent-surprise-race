\begin{abstract}
A video world model that is about to be surprised might reveal it in the
\emph{geometry} of its internal states before its prediction error rises. The
claim is worth testing: prediction error is the established signal by which
these models detect physical violations \citep{garrido2025intuitive}, and
representation geometry is known to carry physical plausibility on its own
\citep{interno2026geophys}. What is not established is which of the two crosses
an alarm threshold sooner --- and that comparison turns out to be unusually easy
to decide in advance by accident.

We present a pre-registered, leakage-free protocol for measuring \emph{detection
latency} in frozen video world models, a synthetic benchmark of four anomaly
families injected at a known frame, and a model-agnostic adapter contract that a
new architecture joins in roughly twenty-five lines and is then held to by a
parameterised test suite. Detection latency is scored as a survival quantity,
with thresholds calibrated on held-out controls so every monitor is matched on
false alarms.

On the registered experiment --- $1000$ anomalous and $200$ control clips
through a frozen V-JEPA 2 --- the verdict is \textbf{negative}, and how it is
negative is the point. Two geometry probes do beat the model's own pretraining
loss significantly, by up to $1.88$ frames of restricted mean detection latency
at Holm-adjusted $p = 0.003$, replicating across two operating points. But a
ridge regression fitted on control clips and reading \emph{exactly the same
latent sequence the probes read} beats all three probes decisively. Since the
gate requires beating both baselines, no probe qualifies. Had we omitted that
information-matched baseline --- as the obvious version of this experiment
would --- we would have reported a clean positive result. The signal is in the
latents; curvature, representational distance and covariance shift are simply
worse ways of reading it out than linear extrapolation.

That baseline is one of several guards we found necessary. The others: a
prediction-error baseline whose context has already attended to the frames it
predicts, making it unfairly strong; a registered primary metric that is
mathematically unable to fire under heavy censoring; a lift check that marks a
passing verdict vacuous when the baseline it beat fires at chance; an occlusion
condition that turns out to measure object permanence rather than partial
corruption; a second architecture that leaks by an entirely different route;
and reduced-precision inference, which degrades the probes roughly thirty times
more than the loss and would have biased the comparison while appearing to be
an implementation detail.
\end{abstract}
