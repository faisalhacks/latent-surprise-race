\begin{abstract}
A video world model that is about to be surprised might reveal it in the
\emph{geometry} of its internal states before its prediction error rises. If
true, that would matter: prediction error is the standard online anomaly signal
for model-based control, and any earlier signal buys reaction time. We test the
claim directly on a frozen V-JEPA 2, with a protocol registered before any code
was written. Three online geometry probes --- trajectory curvature,
representational distance, and covariance shift --- are compared against two
prediction-error baselines, one of which is the model's \emph{own} pretraining
objective evaluated causally. Detection is scored as time-to-alarm under
survival analysis, with thresholds calibrated on held-out control clips so that
every monitor is matched on false alarms.

Our contribution is as much the harness as the answer. Getting this comparison
right turns out to be easy to get wrong in three specific ways, each of which
silently favours one side: a prediction loss whose context has already attended
to the frames it predicts; thresholds fitted on the clips they are evaluated on;
and a censored median that cannot distinguish any two monitors once fewer than
half the episodes alarm. We make each failure mode structurally impossible or
visible, and release the result as a model-agnostic benchmark that a new
architecture joins in roughly twenty-five lines.
On the registered experiment the verdict is \lgwVerdict, with early warners:
\lgwEarlyWarners.
\end{abstract}
